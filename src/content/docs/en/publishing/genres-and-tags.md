Genre and tags are the two taxonomy fields on a story, and both are picked from lists TalePort maintains rather than typed in by you. The genre is the shelf your story stands on; the tags are how a reader who already knows the mood they want narrows that shelf down. A branching horror novella set in a Nordic lighthouse takes Horror as its genre and then Gothic Novel, Nordic, Branching and First Person as tags, which is how it reaches someone filtering for Gothic Novel who has never heard of you. You fill both in when you create the story, and again in the Metadata step of the publish dialog, under **Genre** and **Tags**.

Neither is optional. A chapter cannot be submitted for review until the story has one genre and at least one tag.

## Genre: exactly one

A story has a single genre. There is no second slot, so this is a decision rather than a list.

Pick the shelf a reader would look under, not the one that is easiest to defend. A detective story on a generation ship can go under Crime with Sci-fi and Detective among its tags, or under Science Fiction with Detective among them. Both work. What does not work is choosing Poetry because one page of it rhymes.

The fifteen genres are **Fantasy**, **Science Fiction**, **Horror**, **Thriller**, **Crime**, **Romance**, **Adventure**, **Drama**, **Comedy**, **Historical**, **Mystery**, **War**, **Western**, **Poetry** and **Educational**.

Genre and tag names are translated, so a reader browsing in Czech sees Horor and Gotický román where you see Horror and Gothic Novel. Where a translation is missing, the Czech name is shown.

## Tags: at least one, no maximum

Tags are the finer description: setting, era, tone, structure, who the protagonist is, how it is narrated, what the chapter is made of.

There are 109 of them, each with a Czech and an English name. They cover these kinds of thing:

| Kind | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Subject and form of story | Detective, Romantasy, Dark Romance, Gothic Novel, Fairy Tale, Mythology, Fanfiction, Satire |
| Setting and era | Medieval, Antiquity, Modern Era, Futuristic, Post-Apocalyptic, Urban, Rural, Nordic, Far East |
| Tone | Grimdark, Tragic, Humor, Psychological, Philosophical, Realistic |
| Aesthetic | Steampunk, Cyberpunk, Dieselpunk, Solarpunk |
| Protagonist and narration | Female Protagonist, Multiple Protagonists, Silent Protagonist, Unreliable Narrator, First Person, Second Person |
| Structure and play | Branching, Linear, Skillchecks, Combat, Survival, Roleplay, Riddles |
| Media and format | Audiobook, Video, Animated, ASMR, Comics, Poetry, Short Stories, Novella |
| Audience | For Toddlers, Family, Educational, LGBT |

The picker is a search field rather than a dropdown. Type a few letters, pick the match, and the tag leaves the list and appears under the field, where you can remove it again. Nothing caps how many you add.

A reader who filters on several tags gets every story carrying any of them, not only the stories carrying all of them. So each tag you add is another filter you turn up in, and that is exactly what a loose tag costs you: it puts your story in front of a reader who asked for something else.

TalePort maintains the list and can retire a tag. One that is already on your story stays and keeps working; what you lose is the ability to put it on another story.

## Practical advice

- Accuracy beats reach. A popular tag that does not hold brings in readers who rate the story badly, and a rating is harder to undo than a missing tag.
- Tag what the reader notices, not what you built it from. If nothing in the reading reveals a tag, it is doing no work for you.
- **Tags are not content tags.** A tag here says what your story is about; a [content tag](/en/publishing/content-labels/) warns readers what is in it. Horror the genre, Horror the tag and Horror the content tag are three separate statements, and a horror story usually wants all three.
